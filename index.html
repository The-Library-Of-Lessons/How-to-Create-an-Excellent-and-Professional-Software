    The Great Software Quest

    Building Software People Love and Trust. I will begin with an idea simple enough for a child: software is a tool that promises to help someone. Then, quest by quest, I will travel with you into the decisions made by professional designers, engineers, and software architects.

    How I use the word “great”

    Greatness is a balanced promise

    I do not call software excellent merely because it contains many buttons. I call it excellent when it produces a worthwhile result for real people, makes the result understandable and safe, continues working under stress, respects human dignity, and can improve without collapsing under its own complexity.

    Explain it to a 10-year-old

    A huge toolbox is not automatically a good toolbox. A good toolbox has the right tools, keeps them easy to find, does not hurt the user, and helps finish real work.

    The promise stack

    1. Helpful: it solves a real problem.
    2. Usable: people can understand and control it.
    3. Trustworthy: it is correct, secure, honest, and respectful.
    4. Dependable: it remains fast and resilient when needed.
    5. Sustainable: people can maintain, operate, afford, and improve it.
    6. Adaptive: it learns from evidence without abandoning human judgment.

    This expands the nine-characteristic product-quality model in ISO/IEC 25010:2023 with an explicit starting point: human value.

    Your route through the Software Kingdom

    This is one continuous road. Early quests use ordinary words and familiar examples. Later quests reuse the same ideas with architectural vocabulary, measurements, trade-offs, and enterprise constraints.

    Research synthesis

    The six attached reports strongly agreed on the need for task and schedule management, dashboards, resources, finance, risks, collaboration, automation, security, integration, accessibility, reliability, offline capability, and support. The most mature reports added something deeper: traceability, honest metrics, role-based simplicity, humane monitoring, data ownership, and responsible AI. My guide treats those deeper principles as the roots; features are the branches.

    00
    Explorer · The Gate

    Software is a promise to help

    About 7 minutes

    I think of software as a promise between its makers and its users. The promise says: “Give me some of your time, attention, information, or money, and I will help you reach an outcome.” A calculator promises a trustworthy answer. A messaging app promises connection. A project-monitoring platform promises a clearer picture of work so people can make better decisions.

    This definition immediately improves design. If I begin with screens or features, I can become proud of things that nobody needs. If I begin with the promise, I must ask: Who needs help? What are they trying to achieve? What could harm them? How will I know the help was real?

    Outcome before output
    A released feature is output. A person completing work more safely or quickly is an outcome.
    Context before averages
    A child, an expert, a person using a screen reader, and a field engineer offline can need different paths.
    Evidence before confidence
    Good intentions are not proof. I observe behavior, test assumptions, and measure results.
    Stewardship before novelty
    I protect users' time, data, attention, money, and trust instead of treating them as fuel.

    ISO/IEC 25010 defines product quality around the capability to satisfy stated and implied needs under specific conditions. Its current model covers functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, interaction capability, reliability, security, maintainability, flexibility, and safety. I use those characteristics as a castle inspection, but the user’s real goal remains the reason the castle exists.

    Architect lens: quality is contextual

    There is no single “best” architecture or universal quality target. A pacemaker, a casual game, an internal spreadsheet, and an emergency dispatch system face different consequences. I turn vague wishes into quality scenarios: a named stimulus, operating condition, affected component, expected response, and measurable response target. Later, the Architect’s Council will use this pattern to compare trade-offs.

    Optional Bible reflection

    “Look not every man on his own things, but every man also on the things of others.” — Philippians 2:4, KJV. I can treat software design as service: understanding another person’s burden before showing my solution.

    01
    Explorer · Village of Needs

    Find the real job before building

    About 9 minutes

    I begin discovery with people, not personas invented in a meeting. I watch representative users perform real work, ask about the last time the problem happened, inspect the tools and workarounds they already use, and include people at the edges: beginners, experts, older adults, people with disabilities, slow devices, weak networks, and unusual roles.

    Human-centred design is not a one-time interview. ISO 9241-210 describes human-centred activities throughout the life cycle of interactive systems. I treat discovery as a loop:

    1. Understand the context: people, goals, environment, rules, risks, and current workflow.
    2. State the need: describe the desired outcome without prescribing a feature.
    3. Explore several solutions: include process or policy changes, not only software.
    4. Test small: prototypes, concierge experiments, or a thin working slice.
    5. Measure and learn: compare the result with a baseline and listen for unintended harm.
    Beginner pattern

    Instead of “Users need a dashboard,” write: “Every Monday, a project leader needs to identify the three obstacles most likely to delay delivery in under five minutes, using current evidence.” The second sentence tells me who, when, why, how fast, and what success looks like—without prematurely choosing a screen.

    Use three kinds of evidence

    What people say

    Interviews, support requests, surveys, feedback, complaints, hopes.

    What people do

    Observation, usability tests, funnels, task completion, workarounds.

    What the system shows

    Errors, latency, data quality, reliability, costs, incident patterns.

    Each source is incomplete alone. People may forget details; behavioral data may hide motives; system metrics may miss human frustration. Triangulation—looking for the same truth from several angles—raises confidence.

    Prioritize outcomes, not the loudest request

    I weigh reach, severity, frequency, strategic fit, confidence, effort, risk reduction, and fairness. I also ask what happens if I do nothing. A rarely used accessibility repair can be more important than a popular cosmetic request because the first removes a complete barrier while the second adds mild delight.

    Decision simulation: the “More Reports” request

    A manager asks for 25 additional reports because weekly meetings take too long. What do you investigate first?

    Choose an approach to see its consequence.
    Architect lens: discovery artifacts that survive

    I keep a lightweight evidence chain: opportunity statement → affected users → research observations → assumptions → experiment → result → decision. I give every risky assumption an owner and an expiry date. This prevents a guess from slowly becoming “a requirement” merely because it was copied into many documents.

    02
    Explorer · Bridge of Clarity

    Make the next step obvious

    About 9 minutes

    People should not need to understand the maker’s database, org chart, or technical vocabulary to use a product. I organize the interface around the user’s mental model: the objects they recognize, the decisions they make, and the order in which work happens.

    I use a simple clarity test on every screen:

    • Where am I? The page, project, scope, date, and current state are visible.
    • What can I do? Important actions look actionable; unavailable actions explain why.
    • What happens next? Consequences, progress, saving, and waiting are visible.
    • Can I recover? Undo, drafts, confirmations, and respectful error messages reduce fear.

    Accessibility is part of correctness

    WCAG 2.2 organizes web accessibility under four principles: content should be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust. Conformance is tested at A, AA, and AAA levels. I target Level AA as a practical baseline and test with keyboards, screen readers, zoom, contrast modes, reduced motion, and real people.

    Perceivable
    Text alternatives, captions, contrast, scalable text, and meaning beyond color.
    Operable
    Keyboard access, visible focus, generous targets, enough time, and motion control.
    Understandable
    Plain words, predictable patterns, instructions, and recoverable validation.
    Robust
    Semantic structure and names that assistive technologies can interpret.

    Inclusive design also helps people carrying a child, standing in sunlight, using an old phone, learning English, recovering from an injury, or working while tired. An “edge case” is often a future version of everyone.

    Architect lens: progressive disclosure without hidden power

    I separate essential, frequent work from exceptional configuration. Beginners receive safe defaults and guided actions. Experts receive shortcuts, bulk operations, saved queries, and automation—not a completely different truth. Progressive disclosure reduces cognitive load, but I never hide a consequential state or bury a user’s rights.

    Optional Bible reflection

    “Let all things be done decently and in order.” — 1 Corinthians 14:40, KJV. Order in an interface can be an act of kindness: it lowers confusion and gives attention back to the person’s real work.

    03
    Explorer · Hall of Guidance

    Teach, prevent, confirm, and recover

    About 8 minutes

    A satisfying product behaves like a calm guide. It teaches in context, prevents predictable mistakes, confirms what happened, and offers a path home when something fails. It does not blame the user for misunderstanding the product.

    The four moments of guidance

    1. Before action: good labels, examples, sensible defaults, constraints, and previews.
    2. During action: immediate validation, visible progress, autosave, and interruption safety.
    3. After action: clear confirmation, new state, next step, and an undo window.
    4. After failure: preserve work, explain the cause in plain language, offer recovery, and record enough diagnostic context for support.

    Empty states should teach, not merely announce emptiness. Loading states should preserve layout and explain long waits. Permission failures should say what access is required and who can grant it. Conflicts should show both versions when an automatic merge could destroy meaning.

    Dark-pattern warning

    I reject deceptive design: hidden costs, preselected consent, false urgency, difficult cancellation, privacy choices that shame the user, and confusing buttons that benefit the company at the user’s expense. Trust lost through manipulation is expensive to rebuild. The Philippine National Privacy Commission has published guidance on deceptive design patterns and child-oriented transparency.

    Onboarding should deliver value quickly

    I do not begin with a tour of every menu. I help the reader or user complete one meaningful job, with a sample or safe sandbox if needed. Then I reveal more as their goal requires it. The best onboarding gradually makes itself unnecessary.

    For a beginner

    • Sample project
    • One clear next action
    • Plain glossary
    • Safe practice mode
    • Visible help

    For an expert

    • Import existing data
    • Keyboard shortcuts
    • Bulk editing
    • Command palette
    • Skip or customize onboarding
    Architect lens: make states explicit

    I model loading, empty, partial, stale, unauthorized, offline, conflicting, failed, retrying, cancelled, and successful states—not only the happy path. A state machine or decision table often reveals missing transitions before code does. For consequential workflows, I preserve idempotency so retrying does not create duplicate payments, tasks, or approvals.

    04
    Builder · River of Flow

    Respect time, attention, devices, and networks

    About 10 minutes

    Performance is user experience. A fast server can still produce a slow screen; an average response can hide a painful slow tail; a beautiful cloud feature can become useless on a shipyard LAN or a field device without internet. I measure the experience where users feel it.

    For web experiences, Core Web Vitals provide widely used field measures: Largest Contentful Paint for loading, Interaction to Next Paint for responsiveness, and Cumulative Layout Shift for visual stability. Current “good” thresholds are LCP at or below 2.5 seconds, INP at or below 200 milliseconds, and CLS at or below 0.1, evaluated at the 75th percentile. These are starting points, not substitutes for task-specific goals.

    Simple picture

    The average student may arrive in 10 minutes, but that does not help the five students who were trapped outside for an hour. I inspect percentiles and worst important journeys, not only averages.

    Design for graceful degradation

    • Load the essential task before decoration.
    • Paginate or virtualize large lists; do not freeze the device with 100,000 rows.
    • Compress transfers, cache safe data, and avoid repeating work.
    • Show stale timestamps and sync status honestly.
    • Queue offline writes durably and make retry safe.
    • Never silently overwrite another person’s change.

    Service workers can intercept requests and support cached offline web experiences, while IndexedDB can persist larger structured data in the browser. But “offline mode” is not a checkbox. It needs an explicit data model, conflict policy, security for local copies, migration rules, and tests after long disconnections.

    Architect lens: performance budgets and backpressure

    I define budgets for payload, memory, CPU, latency percentiles, query count, and long tasks around critical journeys. At scale, I add backpressure: queues, limits, timeouts, cancellation, circuit breakers, and admission control. A system that accepts unlimited work until it crashes is not generous; it is dishonest.

    05
    Builder · Fortress of Reliability

    Keep the promise when reality becomes difficult

    About 10 minutes

    Reliability means more than “the server is awake.” I ask whether the system returns the correct result, preserves data, responds within a useful time, survives expected faults, and can recover when prevention fails. A perfectly available system that returns wrong project totals is still unreliable.

    Five reliability questions

    Availability
    Can authorized users reach the service when they need it?
    Correctness
    Does it produce the right state and answer?
    Durability
    Will accepted data still exist later?
    Recoverability
    Can service and data be restored within agreed limits?
    Resilience
    Does one failure stay contained instead of becoming a cascade?
    Operability
    Can people understand, diagnose, and safely change the running system?

    Google’s Site Reliability Engineering guidance separates a service-level indicator (SLI), the measured behavior; a service-level objective (SLO), the target; and a service-level agreement (SLA), a promise with consequences. It recommends choosing a small number of indicators that represent what users care about, such as availability, latency, throughput, durability, and correctness.

    I avoid the fantasy of perfect availability. A sensible error budget makes the trade-off between innovation and reliability explicit. When reliability is comfortably inside the budget, the team can release. When the budget is being consumed too quickly, it invests in stability. The target comes from user and business consequences, not from how many nines look impressive.

    Design for failure before failure

    • Remove single points of failure where consequences justify the cost.
    • Use timeouts, bounded retries with backoff, circuit breakers, and idempotent operations.
    • Separate critical paths from optional features so decoration cannot stop core work.
    • Back up data, define Recovery Point and Recovery Time Objectives, and test restoration.
    • Practice incident response and write blameless postmortems that produce owned improvements.
    • Communicate status and workarounds honestly during an incident.
    A backup is only a hypothesis

    Until I restore it, verify its integrity, and prove the restored system works, I do not know that I have a usable backup.

    Architect lens: observability follows questions

    I collect logs, metrics, traces, and business events that help answer known operational questions. Google SRE distinguishes internal white-box signals from black-box tests of what a user actually experiences. I alert on symptoms that require timely human action, not every internal fluctuation. Every alert should name impact, urgency, owner, evidence, and the next safe diagnostic step.

    06
    Guardian · Citadel of Trust

    Protect people, not merely databases

    About 11 minutes

    Security protects confidentiality, integrity, availability, and safety from deliberate or accidental harm. Privacy asks a different but connected question: even if I can collect and secure information, should I collect it, for what declared purpose, for how long, and under whose control?

    I build both into requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and retirement. NIST’s Secure Software Development Framework provides practices that can be integrated into any development life cycle. OWASP ASVS supplies testable requirements for web application security controls.

    A practical defense sequence

    1. Know what matters: data, money, safety, identity, operations, reputation, and legal duties.
    2. Model threats and misuse: attacker goals, accidental mistakes, insider risks, supply-chain risks, and failure paths.
    3. Reduce exposure: collect less, retain less, expose fewer services, remove default access.
    4. Enforce least privilege: authenticate identity, authorize every action, separate duties, and expire access.
    5. Protect every boundary: validate input, encode output, use safe queries, protect secrets, encrypt appropriate data, and verify dependencies.
    6. Detect and respond: tamper-evident logs, anomaly detection, revocation, incident playbooks, and breach processes.
    Dummy-friendly difference

    Authentication asks, “Who are you?” Authorization asks, “What may you do here?” A valid employee login should not automatically open every confidential project.

    Privacy by design for a Philippine-first public guide

    The Philippine Data Privacy Act’s implementing rules emphasize transparency, legitimate purpose, and proportionality. People should understand the nature, purpose, extent, risks, safeguards, controller identity, and their rights in clear language; collection should be necessary and not excessive.

    Question I askSafer design response
    Do I need a real name?Use an anonymous identifier when the purpose does not require identity.
    Must cloud storage be automatic?Keep progress local by default and offer informed, optional synchronization.
    What if the reader is a child?Use child-friendly language and involve a parent or guardian when consent law requires it.
    Can the reader leave?Provide export, deletion, sign-out, and plain retention information.
    Can one reader see another’s data?Use database grants plus row-level ownership policies, then test them with two accounts.

    This guide follows that pattern: learning progress is local first; cloud backup is opt-in; a publishable key is paired with Supabase Auth and Row Level Security; and readers can delete their cloud account. Supabase explicitly warns that a public publishable key must be protected by RLS and distinguishes an anonymous Auth user from the unauthenticated anon role.

    Architect lens: trust boundaries and secure defaults

    I draw trust boundaries around browsers, services, databases, files, integrations, administrators, and external tenants. Every boundary crossing authenticates, authorizes, validates, limits, logs, and fails safely. I treat client input, uploaded content, webhooks, and AI-retrieved text as untrusted. I never place a service-role secret in browser code.

    Guardian · Library of Truth

    Make every important number explain itself

    About 10 minutes

    A dashboard can be beautiful and still lie. It may combine different definitions, hide stale data, average incompatible projects, or display a percentage that nobody can reproduce. I treat data quality and traceability as product features, not back-office chores.

    The six labels of a trustworthy metric

    1. Meaning: the precise business definition and unit.
    2. Scope: which people, records, projects, dates, and exclusions are included.
    3. Source: where the raw evidence comes from and who owns it.
    4. Freshness: the cutoff time, update frequency, and current delay.
    5. Method: the formula, aggregation, treatment of missing values, and version.
    6. Path: a drill-down from the number to the records that produced it.

    Bidirectional traceability

    Goal
    ↔
    Requirement
    ↔
    Deliverable
    ↔
    Work
    ↔
    Test / evidence
    ↔
    Outcome

    For project monitoring, I keep planned, approved baseline, current forecast, and actual values distinct. If a delayed forecast overwrites the approved baseline, the project appears healthy by erasing the promise. An authorized change may create a new baseline version, but the history and reason must remain.

    Leading, lagging, and guarding measures

    Leading

    Signals that may predict an outcome: aging work, unresolved blockers, review wait, risk response progress.

    Lagging

    Outcomes already observed: missed milestones, defects escaped, actual cost, realized benefit.

    Guardrails

    Measures that prevent optimization from causing harm: workload balance, accessibility failures, data incidents.

    I assume every metric can change behavior. If I reward tasks closed, people may split or close tasks without delivering value. If I rank individuals by hours logged, I may reward overwork and punish efficient problem-solving. I use balanced measures, qualitative context, and human review rather than turning a proxy into a verdict.

    Metric integrity test

    Can the person being measured understand the definition, inspect the evidence, correct errors, and explain important context? If not, the metric is not ready for a consequential decision.

    Architect lens: one source of truth does not mean one database

    It means each fact has a declared authoritative owner and reconciliation rule. A project platform may reference HR, ERP, CAD, and document systems without copying everything. I define data contracts, stable identifiers, lineage, validation, idempotent synchronization, failed-record queues, and ownership for correction.

    Optional Bible reflection

    “A false balance is abomination to the Lord: but a just weight is his delight.” — Proverbs 11:1, KJV. Honest measurement is not merely technical quality; it protects decisions and people from false confidence.

    08
    Guardian · Guild Hall

    Coordinate work while protecting dignity

    About 9 minutes

    Collaboration software is excellent when it puts conversation beside the work, clarifies responsibility, preserves decisions, and helps people ask for support. It becomes harmful when it turns every click, idle minute, or late-night session into a ranking of human worth.

    Context beats scattered communication

    I connect comments, decisions, approvals, files, risks, and changes to the exact project object they concern. A future reader should be able to discover not only what changed, but why, who authorized it, what alternatives were considered, and what evidence supported the choice.

    I distinguish roles clearly:

    • Owner: maintains the item and coordinates movement.
    • Accountable decision-maker: has authority for the final outcome.
    • Contributor: performs or supplies part of the work.
    • Approver: verifies defined criteria at a gate.
    • Watcher or informed party: needs visibility without being assigned the work.

    Design notifications as a scarce resource

    A notification spends attention. I send it only when the recipient can understand why it matters and take an appropriate action. I group duplicates, support digests and quiet hours, respect timezones and leave, let users tune noncritical events, and escalate only when an acknowledged responsibility is at risk.

    Alert simulator

    A project has 300 overdue low-priority tasks, one blocked safety inspection, and a budget threshold approaching in two weeks. What should interrupt the project lead now?

    Choose an alert policy.

    Humane monitoring principles

    • Monitor project health and system conditions, not private behavior by default.
    • State what is collected, why, who can see it, and how long it remains.
    • Use the least intrusive signal that can serve the legitimate purpose.
    • Prefer team-level workload conditions when individual detail is unnecessary.
    • Let people correct inaccurate records and add context.
    • Never let an unexplained algorithm become the final judge of performance, staffing, or discipline.

    The most detailed attached research report made this distinction explicit: monitoring should focus on project health and assistance—not employee spying. I treat that as a defining mark of excellence.

    Architect lens: collaboration needs consistency boundaries

    Real-time coediting, activity feeds, comments, and approvals have different consistency needs. A cursor position may tolerate eventual consistency; a contractual approval may require a transaction, immutable version, identity assurance, and a trusted timestamp. I choose consistency by consequence rather than applying one distributed-systems pattern everywhere.

    09
    Guardian · Automation Woods

    Automate service, not unexamined mistakes

    About 11 minutes

    Automation multiplies a process. If the process is useful, understood, stable, and observable, automation can remove toil. If the process is unfair, confusing, or broken, automation can spread harm faster and make responsibility harder to find.

    My automation ladder

    1. Observe: understand the actual workflow, exceptions, users, and purpose.
    2. Simplify: remove unnecessary steps and approvals.
    3. Standardize: define inputs, rules, ownership, and success.
    4. Assist: suggest, prefill, validate, or draft while a human remains in control.
    5. Automate: execute bounded, reversible, low-ambiguity steps.
    6. Orchestrate: coordinate several systems with retries, audit, and recovery.
    7. Autonomize carefully: only where evidence, authority, monitoring, and stop controls justify it.

    Every automation needs a named owner, trigger, preconditions, permitted actions, limits, idempotency rule, audit log, error route, retry policy, manual fallback, version, test environment, and kill switch. A success rate without the cost of wrong actions is incomplete.

    Responsible AI adds new uncertainty

    NIST’s AI Risk Management Framework organizes risk work around four functions: Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage. It describes trustworthy characteristics including validity and reliability, safety, security and resilience, accountability and transparency, explainability and interpretability, privacy enhancement, and managed harmful bias.

    Show provenance
    Link an AI summary, prediction, or recommendation to the records and time window it used.
    Show uncertainty
    State confidence, assumptions, missing evidence, and known limits.
    Preserve authority
    Require human approval before consequential changes, payments, baselines, or access decisions.
    Respect permissions
    AI may not reveal information that the requesting user could not retrieve directly.
    Test harm
    Evaluate wrong answers, bias, prompt injection, data leakage, automation loops, and unsafe overreliance.
    Make exit possible
    Allow capability opt-out, rollback, manual work, and model replacement.
    Non-negotiable case-study rule

    No AI or automation may silently alter an approved schedule baseline, contract, cost, payment, safety record, or formal approval. It may draft and explain; authorized people decide.

    AI decision simulation

    A model predicts an 82% chance that a milestone will slip, but its top supporting factor is missing timesheets. What should the app do?

    Choose how the system should respond.
    Architect lens: prompt injection is a trust-boundary problem

    Documents, webpages, emails, tool results, and database text can contain hostile instructions. I separate trusted system policy from untrusted content, minimize tool authority, validate tool arguments, isolate tenants, require confirmation for consequential actions, and log the full chain. An AI agent is not a new security principal entitled to broad access.

    Optional Bible reflection

    “If any of you lack wisdom, let him ask of God…” — James 1:5, KJV. Intelligence can propose; wisdom weighs purpose, consequence, truth, and love. I do not confuse faster generation with wiser judgment.

    10
    Architect trail · structure

    Foundry of Architecture: Shape Change Safely

    I design the inside so tomorrow's change does not break today's promise.

    ⏱ 9–12 min

    A house needs rooms, doors, pipes, and wires in sensible places. Software needs sensible boundaries too. Architecture is the set of important structural choices that are expensive to reverse. I do not call a fashionable diagram an architecture. I ask whether the structure makes valuable changes safer, faster, and easier to understand.

    Simple version

    I put things that belong together in the same box. I give each box one clear job. I make boxes talk through small, well-defined doors. Then a change in one box is less likely to smash another.

    Four ideas I use

    High cohesion
    One module holds closely related responsibilities.
    Low coupling
    Modules know as little as practical about one another.
    Stable contracts
    APIs say what goes in, what comes out, and how errors work.
    Replaceable edges
    Databases, vendors, and networks sit behind boundaries I can test.

    A structure that can grow

    → → →
    Select a box to learn its responsibility.

    I start with the smallest architecture that protects the risks

    A well-structured monolith is often the honest first choice. It deploys simply, supports transactions, and lets a small team see the whole. I split a service only when an evidence-backed reason appears: separate scaling, security isolation, independent release cadence, ownership boundary, or resilience. Microservices add networks, partial failure, versioning, tracing, operational cost, and data-consistency choices. Complexity is a loan with daily interest.

    Architect lens: quality attributes are connected trade-offs

    I write scenarios, not wishes: “When 1,000 users submit status within ten minutes, 95% finish in two seconds, no accepted update is lost, and the service recovers without duplicate entries.” I then test architecture against scenarios for security, performance, availability, modifiability, usability, and safety. ATAM gives teams a disciplined way to expose sensitivity points and trade-offs.

    Caching can improve speed but risk staleness. Encryption can add cost but protect trust. Strict consistency can preserve invariants but reduce availability during a partition. I make each choice visible in an architecture decision record: context, options, decision, consequences, owner, and revisit trigger.

    Architect trail · verification

    Testing Grounds: Prove the Promise

    I test the risks people actually carry, not merely the code paths easiest to count.

    ⏱ 10–13 min

    Verification asks, “Did I build it according to the specification?” Validation asks, “Did I build the right thing for real people?” I need both. A perfectly tested button nobody needs is still waste. A valuable payment flow that sometimes charges twice is still harmful.

    My evidence ladder

    Fast rule tests
    →
    Module and contract tests
    →
    A few full journeys
    →
    Real-user observation

    I keep many small deterministic tests and fewer broad, slower tests. But I do not worship a pyramid shape. A data pipeline, embedded controller, design tool, and public website carry different risks. My portfolio follows the system.

    MaintainableArchitecture checks, readable diffs, migration rehearsalChange lead time, failure rate, recovery time
    A common trap

    “100% code coverage” does not mean “100% confidence.” A test can execute every line while checking the wrong behavior. I connect tests to user stories, invariants, threats, and failure modes.

    I release safely

    I use small changes, peer review, automated gates, backward-compatible migrations, feature flags, canaries, and a rehearsed rollback. I watch outcomes after release. DORA's delivery measures help a team balance throughput and instability; I use them to learn about the system, never to rank individuals.

    Architect lens: observability is a question-answering ability

    Logs record events, metrics summarize behavior, and traces connect work across boundaries. I add correlation IDs, structured events, service and business signals, careful redaction, useful retention, and clocks I can trust. I should be able to ask: Which users are affected? Since when? Which change? What dependency? Did we lose or duplicate data? Monitoring should reveal symptoms people feel, not only machines being busy.

    Architect trail · stewardship

    Marketplace of Lasting Value

    I count the whole cost and the whole consequence—not only launch-day applause.

    ⏱ 8–11 min

    Great software must survive economically, socially, and environmentally. If revenue cannot support safe operation, the promise decays. If the business model depends on manipulation, the promise was dishonest. If every small action wastes energy, storage, devices, and human attention, success multiplies the waste.

    Business health

    I connect price to genuine value, know acquisition and support costs, choose a sustainable service level, and budget maintenance.

    Human health

    I avoid addiction tricks, surveillance, forced urgency, and inaccessible support. I make exit and data export real.

    Planet health

    I reduce needless computation and data movement, run work when cleaner energy is available, and extend useful device life.

    Total cost of ownership

    I count design, development, hosting, licenses, observability, security, backups, support, training, migration, compliance, incident recovery, technical debt, and eventual retirement. “Free” software can be expensive if people fight it every day. A costly tool can be good value if it reliably saves greater cost or prevents serious harm.

    Value decision simulation

    A reporting feature is popular with managers, but it performs a huge query every minute even when nobody views it. Which improvement is strongest?

    I design a respectful ending

    Products and features eventually retire. I give notice, explain why, offer migration and export, preserve legally required records, delete what no longer has a purpose, and support people through the change. I do not trap their history to prevent departure. Trust at the exit is part of the product.

    For leaders: a balanced value scorecard

    I review user outcome, inclusion, reliability, security/privacy, delivery health, revenue/cost, support burden, and environmental efficiency together. A metric is a window, not the kingdom. If one target rises while guardrails fall, I investigate the trade-off before declaring victory.

    Earned Value, explained without fog

    PV is the budgeted value of work planned by now. EV is the budgeted value of work actually earned by accepted progress. AC is what that work actually cost. The method can integrate scope, schedule, and cost—but only when baselines, status dates, progress rules, and actual costs are disciplined.

    Architects' Council: Make Trade-offs Visible

    I turn “it depends” into a testable decision with named consequences.

    ⏱ 10–13 min

    Software quality is a system. Strong encryption, instant search, complete audit history, offline editing, global consistency, low cost, rapid change, and simple operations can pull in different directions. My job is not to eliminate every tension. My job is to understand which promise matters, choose deliberately, and watch the consequence.

    My six-part quality scenario

    1. Source: who or what causes the event?
    2. Stimulus: what happens?
    3. Environment: under what conditions?
    4. Artifact: which part receives it?
    5. Response: what must the system do?
    6. Measure: how will I know it was good enough?
    Example

    When a site reconnects after 48 hours offline, the sync service accepts every valid unique update, flags same-field conflicts for the responsible owner, reveals progress within 30 seconds, and loses zero acknowledged changes.

    Council simulation: choose for the context

    Field guide · research synthesis

    The Eight Promises of Great Software

    I studied all six supplied AI research reports as inputs, compared their overlapping capabilities, examined their differences, and checked the central claims against primary standards and official guidance. The reports were strongest as a wide feature inventory; the authoritative sources were strongest as quality, safety, accessibility, privacy, delivery, and governance anchors. My synthesis is this eight-promise compass.

    PromiseChild-friendly questionProfessional evidenceFailure smell
    1. WorthwhileDoes it help someone do something that matters?Observed outcome, adoption in context, guardrailsFeatures ship while the real problem remains
    2. Clear and inclusiveCan different people understand and use it?Task success, accessibility audit, recovery testsPeople blame themselves for confusing design
    3. DependableDoes it keep working and protect my work?SLOs, recovery drills, error budget, offline tests“Usually works” hides lost or wrong data
    4. TrustworthyIs it honest, safe, private, and fair?Threat/privacy models, least privilege, audit, redressConsent theater, silent automation, dark patterns
    5. Truthful with dataCan I tell where a number came from?Definition, lineage, freshness, quality ownerBeautiful dashboards disagree
    6. AdaptableCan good people improve it without breaking it?Modular boundaries, tests, delivery health, ADRsEvery safe change needs a hero
    7. Humane intelligenceDoes automation help people decide without controlling them?Bounded authority, explanation, human review, monitoringOpaque scores alter lives or commitments
    8. Lasting valueCan it keep helping without wasting or harming?Sustainable economics, support, exit, resource efficiencyGrowth depends on lock-in, attention, or hidden cost
    How I use the compass

    I do not average away a dangerous weakness. A product scoring five in visual delight and one in safety is not a “three.” I set minimum trust gates, then improve the weakest promise that limits the important user outcome.
